  ---
  Branch A — Product framing & ambition of v1

  Here's my position, and it's a "think outside the box" one:

  Don't build "a read-aloud web app." Build the engine first, and ship the web 
  app as its first thin client — but resist becoming a multi-client platform 
  until something pulls you there.

  Concretely, v1 is one deployable web app, but the code splits cleanly in two:

  - @reading-engine/core — a framework-agnostic TypeScript package with zero 
  DOM/React. It owns the whole reading state machine: ingest → normalize →
  segment → schedule synthesis → cache → drive a "reading timeline" (which unit
  is active, where playback is). It exposes an event/observer interface. It
  knows nothing about buttons or pixels.
  - The web app — a thin React (or Svelte) client that renders the document,
  subscribes to engine events for highlighting, and forwards clicks (seek) into
  the engine.

  Why this framing wins:

  1. The vision's "platform for many clients" is real, but you get 90% of its 
  value from the factoring, not from building servers/APIs. Extension (phase 2),
  desktop (phase 3), and Delapse all become "just another client" of the same
  core — if the core has no DOM assumptions. Pay that discipline tax now; skip
  the platform-server tax (public API, auth, multi-tenant) until demand is
  proven. That's the YAGNI line.
  2. Your wedge vs. Edge Read Aloud isn't the voice — it's that it's yours and 
  it's everywhere. Edge locks you to Edge. This works in any browser, is open
  source, streams instantly, and (phase 2) reads any selection anywhere.
  Position it as "read-aloud that isn't a hostage to your browser."
  3. It keeps v1 genuinely shippable in weeks — one app, one runtime, no
  backend.

  The one thing I'd explicitly not decide now: the product name. I'd keep
  Reading Engine as the core-library codename and pick a consumer product name
  later (it's cheap to change, and a bad early name is sticky).

  Does this framing land — engine-core + thin web client, flagship web app, 
  platform-factoring without platform-servers? Or do you actually want v1 to be
  more ambitious (a hosted service / public API from day one), or less (just
  ship a web app monolith and refactor later if phase 2 happens)?

● That's the right instinct, and it actually sharpens the model rather than
  copying it. Let me untangle the three things in your message, because one of

  First — you've effectively decided the spectrum, and it's cleaner than "Handy 
  but generous." You want: free = the real features, for individual users;
  forking allowed for tinkerers; but corporations don't get a free ride — they
  pay to embed. That's not maximal forkability and it's not MIT. That's copyleft
  + a CLA + a commercial license, and it's a coherent, common model. One
  clarification so the word "forkable" doesn't mislead us: copyleft doesn't stop
  forking or contributions at all — people can still fork, modify, and share.
  It only stops someone from taking your code closed. So you keep the entire
  community/contributor upside; you just deny corporations the specific thing
  you don't want to give them (a closed embed). That's exactly the line you
  drew. I'll record this as your direction.

  Second — your actual question: do you owe contributors a cut of the money? 
  Short answer: no, not by default — and here's the machinery that makes that 
  clean and fair.

  - The mechanism is a CLA (Contributor License Agreement). Every contributor,
  before their PR is merged, agrees to a short document that grants you (the
  project owner) a broad license to their contribution — including the right to
  relicense it commercially. This is the single thing that makes your
  commercial-embedding-license legal. It's what Qt, MySQL, GitLab, and Sidekiq
  all do. With a CLA, you can sell a proprietary license to a company and
  include community contributions in it, and no contributor has any claim to
  that revenue — because they agreed to those terms when they contributed.
  - Without a CLA, you have a problem. By default, each contributor keeps
  copyright on their lines. If you later want to sell a commercial license that
  includes their code, you legally need every one of their permission. Miss one
  and you can't relicense. So the rule is: CLA (or a "CLA bot" on the repo) from
  commit #1. Don't accept a single external PR without it. (There's a lighter
  thing called a DCO — a "sign-off" — but it only certifies they may submit
  under your license; it does not give you relicensing rights. For
  dual-licensing you specifically need a CLA, not just a DCO.)
  - The cultural norm: the overwhelming majority of open-source contributors are
  unpaid volunteers who get no revenue share and expect none. They contribute
  because they use the tool, want a feature, want it fixed, or want the
  reputation. Maintainers making money is fully accepted — as long as the core 
  stays genuinely free and you're transparent. What triggers community fury is a
  rug-pull (building on volunteer work, then relicensing the open part to
  closed — see HashiCorp/Terraform → the OpenTofu fork, or Redis, or Elastic).
  Selling a commercial license alongside a still-free copyleft core is the
  opposite of a rug-pull and is well-tolerated (GitLab, Sidekiq, Qt all do it in
  the open).
  - Sharing money is optional and strategic, never owed. If you want to, you can
  pay contributors via bounties (pay-per-issue through something like Polar or
  IssueHunt), run a transparent Open Collective budget, or eventually hire your
  best contributors. Projects do this to keep goodwill and momentum, not because
  they must. Start with none of it; add it if and when there's money and it
  buys you loyalty.

  Third — and you're right on the money here: contributions matter most exactly 
  where you said. For the phase-1 web app, honestly, contributions are marginal
  — it's a focused codebase you can hold in your head. The enormous contribution
  value is the phase 2/3 per-site adapter layer, and it's the same pattern that

● Last business decision before we flip to the technical build: which copyleft 
  license (E3).

  My recommendation: AGPLv3, applied to the engine and the apps, backed by a 
  CLA. The reasoning, quickly:

  - Not LGPL — LGPL exists specifically to let companies embed a library in
  closed software without paying or opening anything. That's the exact opposite
  of your line. Wrong tool.
  - AGPL over plain GPL — GPL forces anyone who distributes a derivative to open
  it, but a company that runs your engine as a hosted service never
  "distributes" it, so GPL leaks there. AGPL closes that loophole: network use
  counts as distribution. Since you've floated a hosted synthesis service and
  the vision mentions a public API, AGPL is what stops a competitor from hosting
  your engine closed. For the purely client-side parts it behaves just like GPL
  — so AGPL costs you nothing extra and adds protection exactly where you'd
  otherwise be exposed.
  - Don't split the license (permissive engine / copyleft apps). It's tempting
  to make the engine-library permissive "to encourage embedding" — but that
  hands corporations the free embed you just said you don't want to give, and
  kills your commercial line. Keep everything AGPLv3 so the only lawful way to
  embed-in-closed-software is to buy your commercial license. That single
  license is your business model.
  - Stay OSI-open; skip BSL/source-available. You could go more aggressive with
  a Business Source License (HashiCorp/Sentry style), but it isn't really open
  source, and it forfeits the HN goodwill and contribution flywheel you're
  specifically counting on. AGPL keeps the genuine "open source" badge and
  monetizes corporate embedding. Best of both.

  The one real cost: AGPL spooks some corporate users (a few big companies
  blanket-ban it). But those are precisely your commercial-license prospects —
  so that "chilling effect" isn't lost free users (individuals don't care about
  AGPL), it's your sales funnel. Net positive for you.

  So: AGPLv3 + CLA on the whole repo, trademark the name/logo (Handy's 
  brand-protection trick), donations + corporate-sponsor program from day one, 
  commercial-embedding license as the primary revenue line, premium/BYO-key 
  voices as the one candidate add-on.

  warning was correct, and chasing it down turned up something that saves you

  Correction #1 (the one you flagged): the old docs assumed word timestamps fall
  out of kokoro-js for free. They don't. The public kokoro-js ONNX path gives
  you per-chunk text + audio only — no real alignment. True word-level timing
  requires the timestamped Kokoro model (Kokoro-82M-v1.0-ONNX-timestamped,
  Apache-2.0) plus custom decoding of its phoneme-duration tensors. So the whole
  "highlighting + click-to-seek" premise was resting on an API that doesn't
  exist as described.

  The big find that rescues it — HeadTTS. There's a project
  (met4citizen/HeadTTS) that already does the hard, error-prone part end to end,
  and it's MIT-licensed (explicitly avoids GPL/eSpeak). It:
  - outputs real word-level timing (words, wtimes, wdurations) — plus phonemes
  and lip-sync visemes — using exactly that timestamped model;
  - runs in-browser in a Web Worker, WebGPU-first with automatic WASM fallback;
  - also runs as a Node.js WebSocket/REST server (WebGPU or CPU) — i.e., your
  "local daemon" already exists in the same project;
  - ships on npm/CDN.

  MIT is the perfect license here: clean inside your AGPL app and inside future
  commercial-licensed builds — it doesn't poison your revenue plan.

  Correction #2 (a constraint the old docs underweighted): WebGPU runs Kokoro at
  ~3× real-time; WASM at ~0.5–1× (30s of audio takes 30–60s on a normal CPU).
  So WebGPU-first + WASM-fallback isn't a nice-to-have — on a WASM-only or weak
  device, first-sentence latency is your biggest UX risk, which is exactly why
  the caching system you care about (branch D) earns its keep.

  My recommendation for B1: the engine core defines one interface —
  SpeechProvider.synthesize(chunk) → { audio, words:[{word,start,end}] } — and
  v1 ships a single provider: HeadTTS, in-browser, WebGPU-first/WASM-fallback.
  You do not hand-roll kokoro-js + a custom timestamp decoder + the worker
  plumbing (that was most of the old spec's complexity and its riskiest part).
  Then phases 2–3 and monetization all reuse the same interface: point it at
  headtts-node (the local daemon) for native speed, at a hosted instance for the
  no-install/mobile crowd, or at a cloud premium-voice API for the paid tier.
  That seam is your vision's "provider independence," and it's the exact spot
  your premium-voice revenue plugs in. Streaming stays your job (branch C) — you
  feed HeadTTS one sentence at a time and orchestrate play-as-you-go.

  The honest trade-off: you'd be depending on a single-maintainer project. But

  looking at the wrong thing. That 3D talking head is a separate project (the
  same author's "TalkingHead"), and it's just a customer of HeadTTS. HeadTTS
  itself has no 3D anything — it's a headless audio+timing engine. The lip-sync
  "visemes" it outputs are just numbers in the response you ignore. There's

  What it actually solves is the hard, boring, easy-to-get-subtly-wrong 20% that
  is not your product's value:

  1. Running Kokoro in the browser on WebGPU with WASM fallback, inside a Web
  Worker (all the ONNX-Runtime-Web plumbing and backend detection).

  your commercial-license plan. HeadTTS bundles a non-GPL phonemizer. That's not
  just convenient, it's a licensing landmine they already defused for you.
  3. Decoding the timestamps — turning the model's phoneme-duration tensors into
  {word, start, end}. Fiddly alignment work.

  - Catch 1 — the model must be warm. First-ever load is 7–18 seconds (download

  routine.

  Browser vs. daemon: the answer is both, and this is precisely what the 
  SpeechProvider interface is for — it's not a fork in the road, it's 
  progressive enhancement.

  Your duplication argument is correct and I hadn't weighted it enough:
  in-browser storage is per-profile. A user with two Chrome profiles + Firefox
  downloads that ~85–150MB model three times, warms it up three times, and holds
  three copies in memory. A single native local daemon fixes all of that at
  once — one install, one model on disk, one warm long-running process on a
  localhost port that every browser, profile, extension, and desktop app talks
  to. It's also faster (native runtime, not WASM) and — importantly for your
  warmup worry — it stays warm across all clients, so the 7–18s cold start 
  happens once ever, not once per browser.

  The catch is the opposite one: requiring a daemon install would kill the
  zero-install web wedge (open a page, paste, listen — the thing that makes it
  spread). So:

  - In-browser (WebGPU/WASM) = the zero-install default. The web app just works
  when someone lands on it, no install, ever. This is phase 1.
  - Local daemon = a first-class optional upgrade. When the app detects a daemon
  running (a quick localhost probe), it prefers it automatically — instantly
  solving duplication, warmup, and speed for regulars, power users, and
  weak-device users. Shipped as an optional native install around phase 2, but
  the interface is designed for it now.
  - Hosted = the third provider for any-device/mobile and the paid convenience
  leg.

  All three implement the same interface; downstream (pipeline, highlighting,
  seek, cache) never knows or cares which one answered. So you get the
  frictionless web experience and the daemon's "one warm instance to rule them
  all" — the user chooses their tradeoff, the architecture doesn't have to.

  And note this makes the daemon your cleanest answer to the warmup problem too
  — for anyone who installs it, warmup simply stops being a thing. For the
  pure-web path, warmup is mitigated (pre-warm + cache + progress UI) but not
  eliminated — which is exactly why you're right that it deserves its own
  dedicated session and a cleverer solution. I've parked it as a deferred
  sub-branch (B5), not lost.

● Noted and recorded — HeadTTS is now referenced in the resume pointer as cloned
  at refs/HeadTTS, and I read its actual client source. That reading changed my
  picture of Branch C, so let me share the reframing before opening the first
  question.

  The reframing (good news — it de-risks C): I'd been assuming we'd build the
  sentence-by-sentence streaming pipeline ourselves. We mostly don't have to.
  HeadTTS's client already: splits long text into chunks, synthesizes them, and
  fires them back in guaranteed order one audio message at a time — each
  carrying an already-decoded AudioBuffer plus per-chunk word timings (words,
  wtimes, wdurations in ms). It does not play audio, and it does not touch
  markdown. So Branch C is not "build streaming from scratch." It's the four
  things HeadTTS deliberately leaves to us: the content model, markdown 
  normalization, source-word alignment, and playback/interaction.

  That last-but-one — alignment — is the sharp edge, and it's why I want to
  start Branch C at the data model rather than at normalization or playback.

  ---
  Branch C1 — the content/timeline data model.

  Here's the problem that decides everything downstream. HeadTTS gives back
  words that are its tokenization of the spoken, normalized text. But we
  highlight the rendered document. The moment normalization changes anything —
  1999 → "nineteen ninety-nine", $5 → "five dollars", Dr. → "Doctor", markdown
  **bold** → "bold", a code block skipped — the spoken words no longer line up
  1:1 with the words on screen. If we naively highlight by matching HeadTTS's
  words to the DOM, highlighting drifts and click-to-seek lands on the wrong
  word. The old voiceover spec quietly assumed 1:1; it isn't.

  My recommendation: a source-token-anchored unified timeline as the single 
  source of truth.

  The engine core holds one flat, ordered array of source tokens (the words as
  they appear in the rendered document). Each token carries:
  - a stable id,
  - its rendered surface text,
  - an opaque render anchor the client supplies (so core stays DOM-free — the
  client knows how to map id → a DOM span; core never does),
  - and, once synthesized, audioStart/audioEnd on one absolute millisecond
  timeline.

  Content flows: render → extract ordered source tokens (+anchors) → normalize 
  each segment into speakable text while remembering which source tokens 
  produced it → synthesize → distribute HeadTTS's returned per-word timings back
  onto the source tokens. After that, both highlighting and seeking are just
  lookups over this one array: highlight = "given audioContext.currentTime,
  binary-search for the token whose [start,end] contains it, tell the client to
  highlight that id"; seek = "client reports a clicked id → read its audioStart
  → restart audio there." This is exactly the vision's "reading unit" (logical
  position + visual position + spoken position) made concrete, and it's the
  thing that makes N↔M alignment representable — within a segment, several
  source tokens can share a time span, resolved in C4.

  The alternatives I'm rejecting: (a) audio-timeline-primary (treat HeadTTS's
  words+times as the truth, fuzzy-match to the DOM per frame) — brittle, desyncs
  on any normalization, no stable seek target; (b) segment-only
  (sentence-level, no word tokens) — can't do word highlighting or word-click
  seek, which are hard requirements; (c) re-derive the mapping at runtime by 
  string-matching every frame — expensive and fragile. The token-anchored array
  pays a small upfront extraction cost and makes the rest of the pipeline fall
  out cleanly.

  Does the source-token-anchored timeline as the core's single source of truth
  sound right to you — in particular, are you comfortable that the client owns 
  the render anchors and the core stays DOM-free, talking only in token ids? Or
  do you picture the core reaching into the DOM directly?
